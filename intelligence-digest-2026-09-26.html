01

Money is getting dearer everywhere, and the first place it shows is the debt behind the AI build-out

The single biggest change: long-term borrowing costs are at 20-year highs, and the lenders to AI data centres are the first to flinch.

In short
  • Governments and companies now pay the most in about 20 years to borrow money for a long time.
  • The giant AI data-centre build-out is paid for more and more with borrowed money, and the lenders are getting nervous first.
  • Share prices of the big tech companies have not fallen yet, so the warning is sitting in the bond market, not the stock market.

The price of a long loan is the highest in a generation

A yield is the yearly return a lender demands to hand over money for a set number of years. When yields rise, every loan tied to them gets dearer, from a home loan to a company bond. The US government's 30-year yield closed at 5.49%, its highest in more than 22 years.

  • Intraday high — the 30-year yield touched 5.53% during trading before it closed.

This is not only an American story. Japan's long-term borrowing cost hit its highest level in about 30 years, and Germany's reached its highest since 2009. When the 3 biggest bond markets of the rich world move up together, money becomes dearer for almost everyone who borrows in any of them.

The reason is simple. Oil has been dear since the war with Iran began in February, prices in the US rose 3.4% over the past year, and the US central bank (the Federal Reserve, or Fed) is now raising its rate instead of cutting it. Lenders want more to protect themselves, so the cost of a long loan keeps climbing.

The AI build-out now runs on borrowed money

The 5 biggest cloud companies (Amazon, Alphabet, Microsoft, Meta and Oracle, often called hyperscalers because they run data centres at huge scale) are spending more each year on chips, buildings and power. The line below shows how fast that spending has grown, and how much analysts expect.

Wall Street estimates put their combined spending at about $4.2 trillion through 2029. A growing share of this is raised as debt: company bonds, bank loans, private credit and special companies set up only to own 1 data centre. Bank of America estimates that investment-grade and high-yield bond funds now pay for about half of the AI build-out, so when bond buyers get nervous, the build-out feels it.

  • 2027 alone — more than $1 trillion of that spending falls in 2027.

The first cracks are in credit, not in shares

Oracle sent a "force majeure" notice on Project Jupiter, a 2.45-gigawatt data centre in New Mexico built with Blue Owl Capital. Force majeure is a legal clause that excuses a party when events outside its control, here a delayed gas pipeline for the power plant, stop it from keeping to a contract. Oracle says the project is still on schedule, but lenders reacted fast.

  • Oracle credit insurance — the price of insuring Oracle's debt against default (a credit default swap, or CDS) jumped to a record 237 basis points, more than 4 times the average for investment-grade companies.
  • Oracle bonds — its bonds due 2056 yielded 8% for the first time, the level of a much riskier, junk-rated company.
  • Meta data-centre bonds — bonds from Meta's Beignet data-centre project fell to about 92 cents on the dollar from about 110, trading like single-B debt despite an A+ rating.
  • Oracle shares — fell only about 3% on the news, far less than the move in its credit.
  • Tech CDS — insurance on hyperscaler debt widened 2 to 4 basis points while the Nasdaq 100 stayed flat.

A basis point is one-hundredth of a percentage point, so 237 basis points means paying 2.37% a year to insure the debt. The gap between nervous lenders and calm shareholders is the key tension of the moment, and section 08 looks at whether the AI profits can catch up with the debt.

Why the returns are under question

Goldman Sachs worked out that the hyperscalers need about $1.42 trillion of revenue in the years to 2030 to earn a 15% return on their AI spending. That is only possible if chip rental prices and the price of AI answers (tokens) stay near today's levels. Both are falling: rental prices for older chips are rolling over and token prices are at record lows because cheap open models from China compete hard.

Goldman's own chart shows the return these companies earn on the money they invest has already fallen from about 40% to about 25%. So the build-out is getting bigger while each dollar earns less, which is exactly when lenders start to ask for more.

How the pieces connect, and where India fits

India feels this in 3 ways: dear oil, a weak rupee and foreign investors pulling money out. The Nifty 50 index fell for a 7th week in a row, and foreign investors are on course for their largest yearly selling on record, even though Indian companies' June-quarter profits beat forecasts.

  • Rupee — closed near 95.8 to the US dollar.

The link is not that Indian companies are doing badly. It is that when a US government bond pays 5.5% with almost no risk, money needs a better reason to sit in emerging markets. So the story of this issue is about the price of money first, and everything else second.

02

The hard limits: a strait that stays half-shut, a pipeline that is late, and a grid queue money cannot jump

Physical limits that no amount of money can fix quickly, and who they squeeze.

In short
  • The Strait of Hormuz is still only partly open, and the US turned down Iran's offer to reopen it.
  • Oil tankers are moving again, but mostly because they sail with US navy protection, not because peace is near.
  • For data centres, the scarce thing is not money or chips but a live connection to the power grid.

Hormuz: the offer and the refusal

The Strait of Hormuz is a narrow sea lane between Iran and Oman. Before the war, about 21 million barrels of oil a day passed through it, roughly a fifth of the world's oil. Iran's foreign minister Abbas Araghchi offered, through Qatar, a 7-day plan to reopen it.

  • Iran's terms — a 7-day halt to fighting, release of at least $12 billion of frozen Iranian money, an end to US sanctions on Iranian oil and an end to the US naval blockade of Iranian ports. The strait would reopen on day 7.
  • The US answer — President Donald Trump rejected the plan and, according to US officials quoted by the Wall Street Journal, told aides he expects bombing of Iran to resume after the US midterm elections in November.
  • The sticking point — the US wants the southern route of the strait kept open; Iran accepts only the route agreed with Oman. The US also told Iran it does not control the strait and cannot set terms for it.
  • No nuclear talks — a senior Iranian official said Iran will make no nuclear concessions and the strait stays closed until its conditions are met.

Iranian commentators read the offer as a move timed before the midterms, when a US president most wants cheaper fuel. So both sides seem to expect more fighting, and markets that jump on each "talks" headline are likely to be let down again.

Oil is moving, but under guard

Flows through the strait are back to about 13 to 14 million barrels a day on a 7-day average, near the brief peak of July.

  • Busiest day — about 60 ships crossed in a single day, the most crude moved in a day since July.
  • Escorts — about 40 of those ships coordinated with the US military for protection.

A second chokepoint: the Red Sea

The Houthis, an armed group from northern Yemen, now hold positions on the Red Sea coast, including the town of Mocha and Perim Island near the Bab el-Mandeb strait. Oil passing through that strait more than doubled to about 8.1 million barrels a day as ships avoided Hormuz, so it has become more important just as it became less safe.

  • Yemen — the head of Yemen's Saudi-backed Presidential Council called on citizens to join the army, a sign the government is losing ground.
  • Saudi Arabia, Türkiye, Pakistan — their military chiefs met in Riyadh under the Mecca defence pact, and Pakistan's prime minister condemned a Houthi drone attempt on Makkah.
  • Seafarers — an Indian sailor was killed on the bulk carrier MV Cape Dao in the Gulf of Oman, and another Indian is missing after the tanker El Gaia was hit in Hormuz. India and Liberia set up a group at the UN on seafarer safety.

The power grid: the queue position is the scarce asset

Oracle's force majeure notice came from something plain: the gas pipeline meant to feed Bloom Energy fuel cells at the site was delayed to February after permits were refused. Money cannot speed up a permit or a substation.

A data centre needs power at 1 exact site on 1 exact date. You can sign a power contract, buy turbines and buy chips, but if another project is ahead of you in the grid connection queue, you wait. So the rent in this build-out may end up with whoever holds the connection, not with whoever holds the chips.

  • Texas — its grid operator has 474 gigawatts of projects waiting to connect, and it paused new connections pending an audit.
  • Community push-back — about $170 billion of US data-centre projects have been blocked or stalled by local opposition since January 2024.
  • Delays spread, a little — of US projects already under construction in June, 28% of those started in 2025 and 20% of those started in 2026 now have later opening dates.
  • Scale check — Project Jupiter's 900 megawatts under construction is only about 1% of the 77.6 gigawatts being built in the US, so 1 notice is not a collapse.

Money and rates: a hiking Fed, a buckling housing market, and a rupee that needs more to hold still

What the central banks did, how the bond market answered, and what it costs India.

In short
  • The US central bank is raising rates to fight inflation, and it signalled at least 1 more rise this year.
  • US home builders are cutting prices at a record pace because home loans cost over 7%.
  • India's rupee and bonds are under pressure, and India's own central bank may have to raise rates too.

The Fed is raising, not cutting

The Federal Reserve raised its main rate by a quarter point to a range of 3.75% to 4.00%, with every member voting for it. Its chair, Kevin Warsh, said inflation "is too high, and has been for too long".

How the US is trying to hold long rates down

  • More short-term borrowing — the US Treasury is expected to sell $1 trillion or more of bills (loans of under 1 year) over the next year. Bills now make up 22.8% of its marketable debt.
  • Buybacks — the Treasury has tripled its buybacks of older long bonds, but long rates kept rising anyway.
  • Fed bill buying — the Fed's bill holdings reached about $554 billion; about $355 billion was added over 9 months, more than the roughly $320 billion added around Covid. Critics call this quiet money printing.
  • Interest bill — for the first time since the 1920s, the US government spends more on interest than on its whole defence budget.

Borrowing short to avoid paying high long rates works only while short rates stay low. With the Fed raising, the bill simply comes due more often, so this buys time rather than solving the problem.

Housing is the first thing to bend

The average US 30-year home loan rate reached 7.45%, the highest since 2023. Builders answered with price cuts.

  • Speed — the home loan rate is up 1.5 points in 6 months.
  • Average new-home price — fell $47,700 in a month (9.1%) to $478,700 in August, the largest monthly drop on record.
  • Median new-home price — down 5.8% from a year earlier to $393,700.
  • Mix shift — homes under $300,000 rose to 22% of sales; homes over $1 million fell to 4%.
  • Sales held up — new-home sales rose 6.4% to 684,000 a year, the most since December 2025, because prices fell.

So builders are protecting sales by giving up price. That keeps volumes going but squeezes their profits, and it lowers the value of the homes that back many loans.

  • India's crude basket — the average price India pays for oil rose to $109.76 a barrel in September from $82.04 in July.

The odd advantage: Indian loans are now cheap compared with dollar loans

A point circulating among bankers: a top-rated US company pays around 5.5% or more to borrow dollars, and India, rated BBB, would pay even more abroad. Add the 3.5% cost of hedging the rupee and dollar loans become very dear. Meanwhile state-run Indian banks are lending in rupees at tight rates.

So Indian companies have a reason to repay dollar loans and borrow in rupees at home, which helps explain why bank credit to large companies is growing fast. The flip side is that the RBI has to defend the rupee while rupee borrowing grows, which is harder to do if it also has to raise rates.

Oddly, US companies look healthy on 1 measure: their net interest payments are about 1% of the value they add, the lowest since 1970, because many locked in cheap loans years ago. As those loans come due at today's rates, that cushion will thin out.

04

Commodities and trade: the barrel price looks calmer than the fuel people actually burn

Where the headline price misleads, and who pays or collects.

In short
  • The oil price on screens swings on every talks headline, but real cargoes cost far more.
  • Diesel is the tightest fuel in the world, because the Middle East and Russia both lost supply.
  • Some countries and companies gain from this, such as US oil producers and pipe makers; many others pay.

Paper oil and real oil

Brent futures are contracts traded on screens for delivery later. Dated Brent is the price paid for real cargoes loading soon. Normally they are close. Now real cargoes cost about $123 a barrel while futures trade near $105, a gap of about $18.

  • Real cargoes (Dated Brent) — about $123-125, down from a peak near $145 in April.
  • Brent futures — closed the week near $104-105; they fell about 3.5% in minutes when a TV report said US-Iran talks had entered a "technical stage".
  • US crude (WTI) — about $92 after the same report.
  • India's crude basket — $109.76 in September, from $82.04 in July.

The sources do not agree on the week's exact Brent move: an Indian newspaper put Brent at $98.59, down 11.5% on the week, while US market wraps put the Friday close at $104.30. Futures swung widely on each headline, so the level depends on the hour it was read. What does not swing is the premium for physical oil, which says the real market is still very tight.

Russian companies now lease air-defence systems and hire guards to shoot down drones; their spending on this rose 7 times in the first half of 2026.

  • US pump price — diesel at about $6.48-6.50 a gallon, about $2.80 more than a year ago, hurting farmers and truckers.
  • No export ban — Senator Ted Cruz told refinery bosses the White House will not ban diesel exports; Energy Secretary Chris Wright warned a ban would raise prices. Options now are tax cuts and wider use of tax-free "dyed" diesel.
  • Diesel crack spread — the refiner's margin for turning crude into diesel hit a record of about $100 a barrel in August, against a normal $15-30.
  • Europe — NATO's head Mark Rutte warned that Europe is closing refineries, not building them, and an army at war needs huge amounts of diesel.
  • Germany gas — Germany is considering making utilities fill gas storage to a minimum level before winter after struggling to refill this summer.

So refiners with spare capacity, including Indian exporters of diesel, earn unusual margins, while every business that burns diesel pays. The pain is spread across freight, farming and defence, which is why governments are looking for quick fixes that do not cut supply further.

New routes around the chokepoints

  • Syria to Iraq — Syria began sending gasoline by road to Iraq; 77 tanker trucks have left. The route used to carry Iraqi fuel oil the other way.
  • Iraq — Prime Minister al-Zaidi wants more export ports and energy corridors linking Iraq, Türkiye, Jordan and Syria.
  • Saudi Arabia — its East-West pipeline is carrying about 4.7 million barrels a day around Hormuz, according to McKinsey.
  • Black Sea grain — Russia says it could restart about 80% of its Black Sea and Azov grain export capacity quickly if attacks stop; 3 damaged terminals need months.

Metals: "owning the bottleneck"

  • Tungsten — prices are up about 775% since the start of 2025 after Chinese export controls. China mines about 80% of the world's tungsten. Almonty Industries, which runs mines in South Korea and Portugal, is being covered by Jefferies, Goldman Sachs and Stifel in quick succession.

  • Copper — Apollo notes a new copper mine takes about 18 years from discovery to production, and only 1 or 2 big finds are made a year now.
  • Aluminium extrusion in India — raw material costs rose 30-40% and energy costs about 30% after the Hormuz crisis. Output fell to 0.8 million tonnes a year against capacity of 3 million tonnes.

The Indian extrusion industry expects demand to grow about 10% a year from data centres, solar, EVs and buildings. But its 400 small and mid-sized units cannot pass on costs because imported finished aluminium competes, and tax deductions at source lock up their working capital for months. So demand is there, but margins and cash are not.

  • Cash locked — tax deducted at source ties up working capital for 2-3 months.

Who collects

  • US oil — the US now pumps close to 20 million barrels a day, about twice Saudi Arabia or Russia, and US exports rose about 20% as Asian buyers switched.
  • Canada — oil and gas deals in Canada are heading for about $53 billion in 2026, the most in a decade, including Shell's $16.4 billion purchase of ARC Resources.
  • Pipe makers — Indian pipe makers with US plants are winning record orders; section 06 covers Welspun Corp.
  • Importers — India, Pakistan and small Asian economies pay the premium on every barrel and every tonne of diesel.

A political chart circulated by President Trump counted 24 days of crude above $100 under his current term. That count does not match the physical price of Brent, which has been above that level for much of 2026, so it depends on which price and which days are counted.

What the government did, what the numbers show, and why the market has not rewarded it.

The catch: imports still rise

Phone PLI has been a success, but phones are made mostly from imported parts. Electronics imports are now about 15% of India's total imports, second only to crude oil. The gap between electronics exports and imports has narrowed, but India is not yet a net exporter.

  • Since 2014-15 — mobile exports up about 165 times, mobile production up 33 times, electronics output up 7 times to about ₹13 lakh crore.
  • But — electronics imports also rose about 3.6 times to roughly ₹8.5 lakh crore.
  • Final assembly adds little — assembling a phone adds only 15-20% local value. Making displays, cameras and boards in India lifts that to 35-40%.
  • ECMS — the ₹40,000 crore Electronic Component Manufacturing Scheme now pays companies to make those parts.

So the next test of the policy is not how many phones India assembles but how many of the parts inside them it makes. The companies moving into that harder work are named in section 06.

AI: demand up 18 times, prices down

Goldman Sachs estimates that total AI token demand (tokens are the small pieces of text AI models read and write, and the unit they are sold in) rose about 18 times from December 2025 to September 2026. But demand for the most advanced "frontier" models slowed from July, and cheap open models have pushed average token prices to record lows.

So more AI is being used, but a lot of the growth goes to cheap models. For the hyperscalers, that is the worst mix: huge spending on the newest chips, while customers shift to low-price answers. Goldman's chart shows their realised return on invested capital has fallen from about 40% to about 25%.

Tools for reading the numbers

  • Cash vs profit — a tea-stall example: ₹6,000 of EBITDA (profit before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation) can turn into zero cash if customers owe ₹4,000 and ₹2,000 sits in stock. CFO/EBITDA shows how much profit becomes cash.
  • ROE vs cost of equity — a 20% return on equity is only good if it beats what shareholders need for the risk (say 12%), and if new money can earn the same.
  • Credibility — management credibility "gets accumulated through evidence", not claims.

A friendly summit with small results

Xi Jinping ended a 3-day state visit to Washington. Trump and Xi will meet again at APEC in China in November and at the G20 in Miami in December, making 4 summit visits in a single year, which is very unusual.
